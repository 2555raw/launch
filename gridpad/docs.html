What this actually does.

Short, because there is not much to it: a register with sources on it, a launchpad that is not ours, and one sentence written into every token.

The register

Twenty-eight power stations — seven each of hydro, wind, solar and geothermal — built from the Global Power Plant Database v1.3.0 published by the World Resources Institute under CC BY 4.0. Commercial use is allowed and attribution is required, which is why the attribution is on every page that shows a figure from it.

The database holds 23,354 renewable rows with coordinates, and most of them are called things like PV Plant 118. A register nobody can read through is a search box, so this one is curated. What decides the order is how checkable a row is rather than how big it is: a named authority as the source, a URL on the row, generation actually reported rather than modelled.

Twenty-eight is short enough that being recognised matters too. Ranking purely on verifiability filled the hydro class with installations nobody can picture and left out Three Gorges and Itaipu, so a short list of stations is seeded by hand and the rest is earned. That is a judgement, and it is said out loud in the script that builds the file rather than dressed up as an algorithm.

Nothing in the register is measured here. Every capacity and every coordinate is as its source published it, and that source is linked on the row.

What goes on chain

Pons' token has no field of ours, so the pairing is written into the one public string it does have — its description, set at launch and not editable afterwards by anyone, including whoever launched it:


    
The tag on the end is for a machine. Anyone indexing the chain pulls the ticker straight out
    of it without asking this site for anything, which is the point: the pairing does not depend on
    a database somebody controls.


    
The network

    
Coins launch on Robinhood Chain, an Arbitrum Orbit L2 that settles to Ethereum and
    pays gas in ETH. The pages read that chain whatever your wallet is set to — a wallet parked
    somewhere else does not drag the register to a network with nothing on it.

    

      
      
      
    

    

    
The check only reads. It asks the factory what it is, whether it would take a
    launch from your address and on what terms. Nothing is signed and nothing is spent.


    
Why Pons

    
Launches go through Pons V2, which mints the supply straight into a bonding curve,
    trades it, and graduates it into a Uniswap V4 pool whose liquidity is locked. There is no
    launcher of ours underneath. Writing one and asking you to trust it instead would have been
    the easy thing and the worse one.

    
The launch fee is Pons'. The form reads it off the factory at the moment you are about to
    sign rather than quoting a number hardcoded weeks ago and hoping it still holds.


    
Creator fees

    
Fees accrue on the curve and stay there until a sweep moves them into Pons' escrow. The
    escrow is what pays out, so a balance that looks like nothing is usually a curve nobody has
    swept. The sweep is not privileged: the protocol's operator can call it and so can the token's
    deployer, so a creator never waits on Pons to be paid.

    

      
      
    

    

    

      
      
    


    
What a pairing is not

    
A coin paired to a power station names that station and nothing more. It conveys
    no ownership of the plant, no claim on its output or its revenue, and no physical backing. That
    sentence is not in this footer — it is in the token, and it travels with it into every explorer
    and every wallet that reads it.

    
Nothing on this site is an endorsement either. No exchange, launchpad, operator or public
    body has verified, approved or has any opinion about a coin launched here. The green mark in
    the register means one thing: the capacity and the coordinates are as that named body
    published them, and here is the link.
