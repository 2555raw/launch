How it works

A reservoir is a
bank account for rain.

the thing behind the ticker

A dam does not make water. It holds back what the catchment sends down and releases it on a schedule, which is why the number everyone watches is a level rather than a flow. Here is the whole machine in one section, from the rain to the river below.

  1. 1

    The catchment

    Everything starts as rain and snowmelt on a basin that can be a hundred times the area of the lake itself. The dam collects a whole watershed into one number.

  2. 2

    Live storage

    The water above the intakes: the part that can actually be used. It is reported as a surface elevation, then converted to volume through a curve of area against height that is surveyed for each reservoir individually.

  3. 3

    Dead storage

    The bottom of the basin, below the lowest outlet, plus the silt that has settled into it. It is in the lake but it cannot be drawn, which is why a reservoir at 25% is closer to empty than the figure suggests.

  4. 4

    The intake tower

    A shaft with gates at several heights, so operators can draw from whichever depth has the temperature and the quality they want. Once the surface drops below the top gate, that option is gone for the season.

  5. 5

    Penstock and turbines

    Water falls through a steel pipe onto a runner. Power depends on head, the height of the drop, so a half empty reservoir does not just hold less water: every cubic metre is worth less energy on the way out.

  6. 6

    The spillway

    The relief valve. When inflow beats what the outlets can pass, the surplus goes over the crest and is lost downstream. A spillway running is a sign of a full reservoir, not of supply.

  7. 7

    The compensation flow

    A river below the dam still has to run. Minimum releases for ecology, navigation and the towns downstream come off the top of the account before anyone else is served.

What moves the level

inflow against draw

Storage is a balance, not a forecast: inflow from the catchment, minus releases for supply, irrigation and power, minus what evaporates off the surface and seeps through the ground, plus whatever rain falls on the lake directly. A hot week can take more off a shallow reservoir than a city does.

  • Inflow arrives in bursts, over days, and is the only term that refills the account.
  • Releases are a decision, taken by an operator against a rule curve set months ahead.
  • Evaporation is a loss nobody chose, and it scales with surface area, so it bites hardest on wide shallow lakes.

And in the contract

what Hydropad does with it

Hydropad writes the ticker of one source into the token, where anyone can read it back as source(). The fill figures on this site are the published ones for that source, kept as reference: they are the reason a pairing means something, not an input the curve trades on.

Price comes from the bonding curve alone, which is deterministic and in the contract. A token paired to a reservoir at 31% is a position on a name and the state it is in, and the register keeps that state in front of you instead of leaving it as a logo.
