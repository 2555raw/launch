A paper market, built to a rule

A market where nothing is real except what you do

Perpix lets you bundle three to five assets — stocks, metals, coins, ETFs — into a fixed-weight basket, list it as an index with its own symbol, and take a long or short side on it with up to 5× leverage. The prices are produced by a simulator. That single fact shaped every other decision in it.

Subject Perpix, an index-perpetuals venue Assets 74 Read 6 min

Most trading demos have the same tell. Somewhere behind the chart is a number nobody can account for: a price with no provider, a volume that exists to fill a column, a "0.00" that might be a zero and might be a missing field. Perpix started from the opposite premise. If the market is going to be simulated, say so in the header, say so in the footer, and make everything that is real inside the application actually behave.

What is real, in that sense, is the venue itself. An index you list is listed. It freezes the price of each of its legs, starts at base 100, appears in the market for anyone to trade, and accrues you thirty per cent of every fee paid on it until you claim the balance. A position you open is charged, funded, marked and — if it runs out of margin — liquidated by the rule, on a five-second sweep, whether or not the tab is open.

What an index is here

An index is a fixed-weight basket. Not a strategy, not a rebalancing rule: a list of three to five assets and the weight of each. When it lists, the price of every leg is photographed and stored inside the index, and the basket is defined as base 100 from that moment.

V(t)  =  100  ·  Σi  wi  ·  Pi(t) / Pi(t0)
w the leg's fixed weight P(t) its price now P(t₀) its price when the index listed

Because the reference prices live inside the index rather than in the feed, listing a new index cannot rewrite the history of an existing one, and a basket's chart measures exactly what that basket has done since it existed — nothing more.

One consequence is worth pausing on. Since the weights never move, the index's whole change decomposes cleanly: each leg's move times its weight, and those contributions sum to the index's move. The app shows that column, so a basket that is up 14% will tell you which of its four legs put the 14% there. There is no residual, no tracking error, nothing to explain away.

The one rule

Prices come from a simulator built into the page, and the application says so before it starts: the terms you have to accept say the balance is not money, the prices are not prices, and no order reaches a real venue. The footer of every screen repeats it. Nothing is hidden in the small print, and nothing pretends otherwise.

Within that, the codebase keeps one distinction rigidly, because it is the distinction that makes the rest trustworthy.

Simulated

Everything the market says

  • Every asset price, and every index value derived from one
  • 24-hour and 7-day change
  • Volume, and a basket's reference depth
Real, inside the app

Everything you did

  • Your balance, positions, entries and closes
  • Fees you paid, and fees you earned as a creator
  • The indices you listed, and their frozen references

Simulated figures are marked sim. where a column has room for it. The two are never mixed in one number.

The simulator itself is a sum of waves rather than a random walk, and that is not an aesthetic choice. Waves with phases seeded from each symbol give three properties a walk cannot: the same seed produces the same market on every reload and in every tab, so a position opened yesterday still makes sense today; the price is continuous, so there are no jumps between sessions; and it can be evaluated at any instant in constant time, which means a chart's history is never stored, only computed.

When a real data provider is eventually wired in, it replaces four functions and sets one flag — and at that point the header and footer stop saying the prices are simulated, because they will not be.

Every asset gets its real mark

This is the part that took the most work and reads as the smallest detail. Each of the 74 assets carries its symbol, its real brand colour and its real mark, and that same trio is used on every screen, so an asset cannot show one identity in the search box and another inside a basket.

Nothing is drawn, generated or approximated, which turns out to be a constraint with teeth: it means some assets simply cannot be given a logo, and the honest thing is to show something else that is true rather than something invented. Three tiers, in order of preference.

Where a mark belongs to a brand of the listed entity rather than the entity itself — Google for Alphabet, Chase for JPMorgan Chase, Zara for Inditex — the asset's page names the brand, instead of quietly passing one identity off as another. Every mark also records the set and licence it came from.

A monogram is not a mark. Neither is a logo you drew yourself and hoped nobody would check.

The same principle settled a contrast bug that only surfaced when a dark theme went in. The ink over a brand colour used to be picked by a luminance threshold, and one threshold is wrong at the boundary in both directions: gold sat just under it and took white lettering at 2.1 : 1, which is unreadable. Measuring both candidates and taking the better one cannot be wrong at the boundary. The worst tile in the registry is now 4.5 : 1.

Funding scales with the imbalance between longs and shorts on that index, computed from the positions that actually exist. On a liquidation the margin is lost and no closing fee is charged.

How it is built

No framework and no build step: HTML, CSS and fifteen native ES modules. The identity layer is one file with one row per asset; the feed is one file; the rules are one object; the marks are one generated file that records where each came from. A screen is a function that returns a node, so the router knows nothing about a screen's insides and one screen cannot write into another.

There is exactly one build, and it exists for a narrow reason: native modules are fetched over HTTP, and a page opened from the filesystem has no HTTP. A script inlines the styles and the modules into a single 300 KB file you can double-click, each module still in its own scope so a stack trace names the function you wrote. Forty-seven checks cover what cannot be wrong — that a basket starts at its base, that leg contributions sum to the index's change, that the balance reconciles after every open, close and claim, and that each limit refuses what it is supposed to refuse.

The clock is worth a line. A market has one wall clock, not one per viewer, so the venue publishes its own — currently Paris, named as a zone rather than an offset, so daylight saving is the platform's problem and is right twice a year without anyone remembering the dates.

What it is not

Market orders only: no limits, no stops. A close is the whole position. Baskets never rebalance, which is the property that makes an index auditable without storing history, but it does mean there is no drifting-weight mode. History keeps the last two hundred closed trades. And the balance is not money in any sense — it cannot be deposited, withdrawn or exchanged for anything, because there is nothing behind it.

What Perpix is, then, is a venue you can hold in your hand: a market small enough to read end to end, honest about which of its numbers describe the world and which describe only itself.

Perpix is a paper market. Its prices come from a simulator and do not describe the real market. Nothing in it, or in this article, is investment advice.
