RENTA

A vault that owns a share of rented apartments, publishes what every one of them paid, and lets you check the arithmetic.

Tenants pay rent. The bills come out of it. What is left stays in the vault and lifts the price of a share. You never receive a rent transfer, and you never own an apartment.

On the first of every month the vault publishes The Roll — the report for the month just ended. It lists every apartment, what its lease says, whether the rent arrived, and every cost that was taken out. The file is hashed and the hash is written on chain beside that month's closing share price, so the document you read is provably the document that was published.

These docs cover three things:

  • Using the vault — what a share is, how its price moves, what it costs, and what can go wrong.
  • The Roll — how to read a monthly report and how to verify one yourself.
  • Building on it — the contracts, the chain, the audit, and how to run the whole thing on your own machine.

How it works

The vault is a holding company with a token in front of it. The company — a Dutch B.V. — owns ten apartment buildings outright, in eight cities across six countries, and it owns a cash reserve in EURG. The token, vRENTA, is a claim on all of it in proportion.

Each month, three things happen, in order:

  1. Rent is collected. The managing agents in each city collect rent from seventy-seven apartments and forward it, net of their own fee, to the vault's operating account.
  2. The bills are paid. Repairs, property tax, insurance, the agents and the auditor come out of that account. Whatever is left is kept.
  3. The close is written. On the last day of the month the kept amount is added to the vault's holdings, the new share price is computed, and the Roll is published with its hash on chain.

There is no borrowing anywhere in the structure. No mortgage on any building, no credit line at the vault, no leverage in the token. The vault grows at the speed of deposits and retained rent, which is slower than a bank would let it, and that is the point.

What sets the share price

The price of one vRENTA is what the vault holds, divided by the number of shares in issue. What the vault holds is the sum of two things: the EURG in its reserve, and the buildings at the value they were bought for, adjusted only when a building is sold or an independent valuation is commissioned — which happens once a year, and is itself published.

Because the buildings are carried at cost between valuations, the price moves for exactly two reasons between closes:

  • Rent kept. The month's collected rent, less the month's costs, is added to the reserve.
  • The curve tax. Anyone redeeming within ninety days of a deposit sells slightly below the share price. The difference stays in the reserve for everyone else. See Fees.

Depositing and redeeming

The vault is an ERC-4626 contract, so it works the way every other one does. You approve EURG, you call deposit, and vRENTA arrives in the same transaction at the share price standing at that block. The minimum is 100 EURG. There is no maximum, no whitelist and no lock-up.

To leave you call redeem with the number of shares. EURG arrives in the same block as long as the reserve covers it — and the reserve is kept at roughly a month of rent plus whatever redemptions are usual, which was €430,593 at the last close. A redemption larger than the reserve is queued and settled at the next close, at that close's price. It is the one case where you wait, and the Roll records every queued redemption and the day it settled.

You never receive rent. Nothing is streamed, airdropped or claimable. The rent is in the price. If you want it in your wallet, redeem the part of your holding that is worth what you want.

Fees

Deposit0.00%
Redeem0.00%
Curve taxOn redemptions within 90 days of the most recent deposit, on a straight line from 3.0% on day one to 0.0% on day ninety. It is not a fee we collect: it stays in the vault and lifts the price for the holders who stayed.
Management0.35% of what the vault holds, a year, taken monthly out of rent before the close. It is inside the €18,250 of costs above, and it is a line in every Roll.
PerformanceNone.

The curve tax exists so that a nervous week cannot force the vault to sell a building to pay people who arrived three weeks ago. Buildings take months to sell and sell badly in a hurry; the tax makes leaving early cost about what a hurried sale would have cost everyone else.

What can go wrong

Property is slow and it is not free of risk. In roughly the order you should worry about them:

  • A tenant stops paying. The agent chases it, the Roll records it as scheduled but not collected, and that month's price rises by less. Across seventy-seven apartments one empty flat costs a few hundred euros a month, not everything.
  • A big repair. A roof, a boiler, a façade. May 2026 was one: €4,180 on Kurt-Eisner-Straße, paid once, out of that month's rent. The price still rose that month, by a little less.
  • A valuation falls. Once a year the buildings are revalued independently. If a market has fallen, the price of a share falls with it on that close. This is the only way the price goes down other than a redemption queue being settled below reserve.
  • A redemption queue. If redemptions exceed the reserve, they wait for the next close. In an extreme case the vault would sell a building to meet them, at whatever the market gives, and everyone's share would carry that.
  • The stablecoin. The vault holds EURG. If EURG were to trade away from one euro, the reserve would be worth less in euros even though nothing about the buildings changed.
  • Us. The company that runs the vault could stop. The holding company and the buildings do not depend on it: vRENTA holders own the holding company, the vault contract accepts redemptions from any wallet whether or not this site exists, and the deeds are registered in the ordinary way in each country.

Reading a Roll

A Roll is one month of the vault, in full, in one file. It has four parts, and they are always in this order:

  1. The header. The month, the opening and closing share price, the shares in issue at open and close, and the hash of the previous Roll.
  2. The rent roll proper. One line per apartment: building, unit, what the lease says is due, what arrived and on which day, and a note if it was late, partial or missing.
  3. The costs. Every payment out of the operating account, with the building it belongs to, the payee, the invoice reference and the amount. The management fee is a line here like any other.
  4. The movements. Every deposit and redemption in the month, by wallet, with the price it was struck at and any curve tax applied.

The two numbers people read first are scheduled and collected. Scheduled is what the leases say. Collected is what arrived. The gap between them is the month's story, and the rent roll tells it line by line.

Verifying a Roll

A Roll is published as a plain file, and its SHA-256 hash is written to the vault contract by the same transaction that sets the month's closing price. To check one: 

# fetch the Roll for August 2026 and hash it
curl -sO https://renta.example/rolls/2026-08.csv
sha256sum 2026-08.csv

# read the hash the registry holds for that close
cast call $REGISTRY "rollHash(uint256)(bytes32)" 202608 --rpc-url $RPC

# they should match. If they do not, the file you have is not the one published.

# or let the script do all six, and recompute every close while it is at it
node scripts/recompute.js --check

Then recompute the close. Sum the collected column, subtract the costs, divide by the shares in issue at the close, add the curve tax per share from the movements, and add the result to the opening price. You should land on the closing price in the header to six decimals. A small script that does this for every Roll since the vault opened is in the repository.

The published file format

One CSV per month, UTF-8, comma separated, with a section column that takes one of header, rent, cost or movement. Amounts are in EURG with six decimals and no thousands separators. Dates are ISO 8601. Wallets are checksummed. Nothing in the file is derived from anything outside the file except the previous Roll's hash. 

section,building,unit,lease_due,collected,collected_on,note

movement,,,,,2026-08-21,redeem 0x3f…9a1c 12000.000000 shares at 1.023114 curve 0.42%

Run it locally

The site is plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no build step. The contracts are Foundry. Everything on the site is computed from the Rolls, so with the Rolls on disk you can reproduce every number on it without touching the chain. 

git clone https://github.com/renta-estate/vault
cd vault

# the site
python3 -m http.server 8000        # http://localhost:8000/site

# the contracts
forge build && forge test

# recompute every close from the Rolls
node scripts/recompute.js rolls/

The contracts

There are three, and they are small on purpose.

  • Vault. An ERC-4626 with two additions: a curve on redeem that looks up the caller's most recent deposit, and a close function that only the operator can call, once a month, which sets the new price and the Roll's hash together. The price cannot be set without a hash, and a hash cannot be set without a price.
  • Roll registry. A map from month to hash, append-only. Once written, a month's hash cannot be changed by anyone, including us.
  • Operator. A two-of-three multisig that holds the right to call close. It has no right to move EURG out of the vault; only redeem can do that, and only to the redeeming wallet.

The source is in the repository, verified on the explorer, and it does not upgrade. If a change is ever needed it will be a new vault, announced in a Roll, with a migration you opt into.

The audit

Two things are audited, by two different people. The contracts were reviewed by an independent smart-contract firm before deployment, and the report is in the repository with every finding and what was done about it. The company's accounts — the buildings, the rent, the bank balances — are audited annually by a registered Dutch auditor, and the audited accounts are published beside the December Roll. The Roll's job is to make the second audit boring: by the time the auditor arrives, twelve months of the ledger have already been public and hashed.

Who can invest

vRENTA is offered to people resident in the EU and EEA, Switzerland and the United Kingdom, who are 18 or over and who pass an identity check. It is not offered to residents or citizens of the United States, Canada or Japan, nor anywhere that would require a licence the company does not hold, nor to anyone on an EU sanctions list. The list is in config.js and on the gate you see before connecting a wallet.

The identity check is run by a regulated provider and happens once, before your first deposit. The vault contract itself does not know who you are — it knows a wallet — and the company keeps the link between the two under EU data-protection law, for as long as anti-money-laundering rules require and no longer.

What this is, and is not

vRENTA is a share of a company that owns buildings. In most places that makes it a security, and it is offered as one. It is not a deposit, it is not insured, and its price can fall. Rent can stop arriving. A building can be worth less next year than it was this year. Past closes are a record, not a promise.

Nothing on this site or in these docs is advice. If you would not buy a share of a small property company on the strength of its published accounts, do not buy this on the strength of a Roll.

This is a demonstration build. Every address, every building and every close on this site is invented to show how the mechanism reads. There is no wallet connector, no deployed contract and no company behind it.
